Roofing website design in Melbourne: slate, terracotta and four seasons in a day

Melbourne roofers deal with Victorian slate in Fitzroy, terracotta in the bungalow belt and new Colorbond in the growth corridors. A good website shows which of those you are best at.

Short answer

A Melbourne roofing website should make your specialty obvious, whether that is heritage slate and terracotta or new metal roofs, and back it with photos from the suburbs you cover. Add pages for the leaks people search for, show your registration details, and make calling easy on a phone.

Melbourne has a wide range of roofs. Within half an hour you can go from Victorian terraces with slate roofs in Carlton to 1920s bungalows with terracotta in Coburg to brand-new estates in Craigieburn where every roof is Colorbond. Roofers here usually specialise, even if their website does not say so.

A roofing website here should make that specialty clear first.

01Should a Melbourne roofer specialise on their website?

A homeowner with a leaking slate roof in Fitzroy does not want the same roofer as someone getting a new metal roof on a house in Werribee. If your site lists every service with equal weight, both of them will wonder whether you really know their job.

Pick the work you most want more of and lead with it. If that is heritage slate and terracotta restoration, put your best before-and-after photos on the homepage and write a page explaining how you match old tiles and handle heritage overlays. If it is new metal roofs, lead with that instead. The other services can still have their own pages.

02How should you cover Melbourne's suburbs?

Melbourne sprawls, and travel time matters to both you and the customer. A few service-area pages that name real jobs work better than a long list of suburbs:

  • Inner north and inner east: Fitzroy, Carlton, Brunswick, Richmond, Hawthorn. Terraces, heritage controls, tight access.
  • Bayside and the south-east: St Kilda, Brighton, Moorabbin, Dandenong. A mix of interwar homes and post-war brick veneer.
  • The eastern suburbs: Box Hill, Ringwood, Croydon. Lots of concrete and terracotta tile roofs due for restoration.
  • The western and northern growth areas: Werribee, Point Cook, Craigieburn, Mernda. New builds, warranty work and extensions.
  • Frankston and the Mornington Peninsula, if you go that far. Coastal exposure and holiday homes.

Put a real job on each page. A photo from a re-roof in Box Hill, with a sentence on what you found when you lifted the old tiles, does more for that page than keyword-heavy copy.

03What does Melbourne weather do to roofs?

"Four seasons in one day" is a cliché, but Melbourne weather does change fast. Melbourne roofs deal with heavy rain, strong northerly winds before a cool change, and occasional severe hail. The Christmas Day hailstorm in 2011 damaged thousands of homes across the northern and western suburbs, and hail has hit the city several times since.

The calls that follow are mostly about leaks: cracked tiles, failed pointing on ridge caps, blocked valleys and overflowing box gutters on terraces. Your site should have a page for each of those problems, written the way a homeowner would describe them. "Water coming through the ceiling after rain" is what people search for. "Ridge capping re-bedding and pointing" is what you do about it. Use both.

04Common problems on Melbourne roofing sites

  • No mention of heritage work, even from roofers who do a lot of it. Heritage homeowners search specifically for it.
  • Gallery photos with no captions. A caption with the suburb, roof type and job makes each photo work for search.
  • Contact pages that only offer a form. Many people still want to call, especially for a leak.
  • Reviews pasted in as images, which Google cannot read. Use text, or embed your Google reviews properly.
  • Old sites built on page builders that load slowly on mobile. Most visitors arrive on a phone.

05The pages that do the work

For a Melbourne roofer, I would start with these pages and add more only when there is something real to put on them:

  • Homepage with your main specialty, service areas and phone number up top.
  • Service pages for restoration, re-roofing, repairs, gutters, and heritage work if you do it.
  • Problem pages: roof leaks, cracked tiles, hail damage, box gutter overflow.
  • Four or five area pages with local jobs.
  • A gallery and an about page with your registration details and team.

06Common questions

Should my site show my registration or licence details?+

Yes. Victorian homeowners are encouraged to check that roof plumbers are registered, and showing it clearly saves them a step and builds trust before the first call. Put the registration number in the footer and on the contact page, so it is easy to find before anyone calls.

Can a new website help with heritage jobs?+

It can, if the site has real heritage work on it. A page with photos of slate or terracotta restorations you have done, and a plain explanation of how you handle heritage overlays, is what those homeowners look for.

Is slate roof restoration worth a separate page?+

Yes, if it is work you want more of. Slate owners often search for it by name, compare carefully and want to see photos of slate jobs. A general restoration page may not be enough to convince them.

How many photos should a roofing gallery have?+

Enough to show your range, usually twenty to forty good ones. Caption each with the suburb, the roof type and one line on the job. Twelve captioned photos are more useful than a hundred unlabelled ones.

What should my contact page include?+

Phone, email, the areas you cover, your registration details and a short form with photo upload. If you have a usual response time, put that there too. A visitor should see at a glance how to reach you and which parts of the city you cover.
